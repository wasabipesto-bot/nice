## B. Join inner-loop acceleration

Hot loop: ~1,200 tops/prefix (64-bit mask, 16–18 bits set) against buckets of ~300
bottoms (64-bit mask, 12 bits set), emitting AND=0 pairs (1–2 % pass); 0.58 ps/pair
in waves of 1024, 74 ps/passing pair.

- **Signature files / bit-sliced indexes** (COBS; bit-sliced signature partial
  evaluation): built to turn an *expensive* object comparison into a cheap bit test,
  with an expensive verification step saved for signature hits. Our bottom mask
  already **is** the exact signature — no hash, no false positives, no verification
  step to skip. The classic win (avoid the expensive check) doesn't exist here; only
  a constant-factor ALU-count change is on the table.
- **Set containment / similarity joins** (Mamoulis, "Set Containment Join
  Revisited," [1603.05422](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.05422); Mann & Augsten,
  "Bitmap Filter," [1711.07295](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.07295), GPU port up to
  577× over a naive CPU baseline on a GTX 980 Ti): both are prefix-filter +
  rarest-element-first schemes over collections of 10⁵–10⁷ variable-size sets.
  Speedup comes from pruning huge candidate counts before an expensive
  verification; at our bucket size (300) and already-trivial per-pair cost, the
  regime doesn't match — *unverified whether the ported technique still wins at
  this scale, no source ran it this small*.
- **Set-trie / BSD-style subset–superset index** (Savnik): O(c·|set|) for subset
  queries, reported constant ≈150 for superset queries (web summary only, original
  PDF blocked by a 403 — **unverified**, not read in full). Pointer-chasing trie
  traversal; not warp/SIMD-friendly, and built for large heterogeneous set
  collections, not fixed-width 64-bit masks.
- **Rare-element / prefix-filter reordering** (PPJoin-family): sort set elements by
  global rarity, process rarest first to tighten a *similarity* bound and cut
  inverted lists short. Doesn't transfer as an early-exit here — exact disjointness
  has no monotone threshold to stop on early, unlike a Jaccard cutoff. The
  *partitioning* half of the idea does transfer (next item).
- **Bit-sliced transposed OR-column** (signature-file idea turned sideways,
  not found verbatim in the literature but implied by it): once per bucket
  (reused across all ~1,200 tops), build 64 small bitmaps — one per digit value —
  marking which of the ~300 bottoms contain it (≈5 64-bit words each, ≈2.5 KB,
  fits shared memory). For a top with 16–18 set bits, OR together the ≤18
  relevant per-digit bitmaps; the result's zero bits are exactly the passing
  bottoms. Replaces ~300 AND+testz ops with ≤18 OR ops over 5-word rows — roughly
  a 15–20× cut in raw op count per top, branch-free and warp-uniform. Whether this
  beats the current 0.58 ps/pair — which already looks issue-rate- or
  bandwidth-bound rather than compute-bound at this size — is **not established,
  only estimated from op counts**.
- **Static rare-bit bucket partitioning**: pick the 1–2 globally rarest digit
  values in a bucket; split the bucket by presence/absence once (reused across all
  ~1,200 tops in the prefix). Each such bit, if set in a top, skips ≈12/64≈18.75 %
  of the bucket unconditionally, for zero added per-pair cost. Two to three levels
  (a shallow static signature/BSD-style tree) could statically remove roughly
  30–45 % of the bucket before the inner loop runs, amortized to near zero given
  ~1,200 reuses per prefix.
- **Bloom-style partitioning by digit presence**: subsumed by the above — our
  masks are already exact, so hashing would only lose information at these sizes.

**Conclusion:** two ideas are cheap enough to measure rather than assume — (1) the
bit-sliced transposed OR-column, (2) static 2–3-level rare-bit partitioning of each
bucket, amortized over its ~1,200 reuses. Both are branch-free, fit GPU shared
memory, and exploit bucket reuse; neither is validated at our scale by any source
found. Every large reported speedup in this literature (up to 577×) comes from
pruning collections many orders larger than our ~300-entry buckets with a much
costlier per-comparison baseline than ours. Read plainly, the literature implies
brute force is *probably* already close to optimal at these sizes — but "probably"
is doing real work in that sentence, and a same-day counting experiment on (1) and
(2) would settle it cheaper than continuing to guess.
