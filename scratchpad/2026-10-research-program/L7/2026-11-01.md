## B. Targeted questions

### B1. Survivor compaction at ~1–2% AND-mask density; filter ordering by cost/rejection

- **Ordering filters by cost ÷ rejection-power is proven optimal**, not just a heuristic:
  Boyd & al. (title not independently verified beyond arXiv listing), *"Optimality of
  Sequential Filtering Under Independent Cost and Selectivity Models"* (arXiv:2606.07589,
  cs.LG, 2026-05-28): under an independence model, ordering stages by **increasing ratio of
  cost to rejection probability minimizes expected total cost**, and this strictly dominates
  both "cheapest first" and "most selective first" heuristics. Directly answers the second
  half of the question — cite this for the prefilter/compaction ordering decision. It's a
  general ranking-pipeline result, not GPU-specific; applicability to a fixed small pipeline
  (mask AND → mid-digit prefilter → compaction) is a direct fit for the model's assumptions
  (independent stage costs/rejection rates) but **not validated by the paper at these specific
  rates** — unverified as applied.
- **Ballot+prefix-sum vs. shared atomics**: no paper found benchmarking these two specifically
  at ~1–2% survival density. The standing answer in the GPU literature is the hybrid NVIDIA
  describes in "CUDA Pro Tip: Optimized Filtering with Warp-Aggregated Atomics" (NVIDIA
  devblog, long-standing, still the reference as of a 2026 reprint found in search): have each
  warp use `__ballot_sync` + `__popc` to compute its own survivor count, then issue **one**
  atomic per warp instead of one per surviving thread. This dominates both pure
  shared-atomics (contention scales with survivor count) and naive per-thread ballot+scan
  (redundant cross-warp work) at low-to-moderate density, which matches your regime. This is
  not new literature and not quantified at exactly your density — treat the specific ~60ps/
  survivor number as uncharacterized by any source found.

### B2. Faster leading-digit extraction than long division by b^k in 16-bit limbs

- **Barrett reduction** is the standard technique for "divide by a fixed modulus using only
  multiply+shift+small correction," and is explicitly used on GPUs to extract a required digit
  "essentially at no cost (without division)" in modular-exponentiation/NTT contexts (found
  via GPU RSA/NTT implementation search, e.g. an NVIDIA-tensor-core NTT paper). This is the
  concrete candidate for your u32-limb, no-bare-half-width-shift constraint: precompute a
  fixed-point reciprocal of b^k once, multiply, shift, then at most 1–2 correction
  subtractions — no division instruction at all, and the shift amount is a compile-time
  constant tied to the reciprocal's precision rather than a half-width shift of the dividend,
  which should sidestep the Metal compiler bug as you've characterized it. **Unverified**: I
  found no paper applying Barrett-style reciprocal extraction specifically to "leading digits
  of a² / a³ for certification," only to modular reduction; the correction-step count and
  precision needed for *exact* (not merely correctly-rounded) leading digits would need to be
  worked out and tested, not taken off the shelf.
- Two GPU multi-precision division papers turned up — "On GPU Implementation for
  Multi-Precision Integer Division" (arXiv:2606.06386, Newton/shifted-inverse, CUDA, targets
  2^15–2^18-bit operands) and "High-Level Big Integer Arithmetic in Futhark for GPUs"
  (arXiv:2607.28897, similar size range). Both are **size-mismatched** to your problem (your
  operands are presumably far under 2^15 bits); their Newton-iteration reciprocal approach is
  the same family as Barrett but tuned for bulk multi-precision ops, not single-digit
  extraction from a short operand. Not a direct fit; noted for completeness only.
- A CPU-SIMD paper, "Converting an Integer to a Decimal String in Under Two Nanoseconds"
  (arXiv:2604.26019), replaces division with SIMD multiply-add on AMD/Intel — same spirit as
  Barrett but no division at all; CPU-only, not GPU/Metal, so not directly transferable, but
  confirms multiply-based digit extraction beating division is an active, current technique.

## C. Adjacent-field changes worth a counting experiment

"Over the Memory Wall, Into the Instruction Wall: The New Bottleneck in GPU Data Processing"
(arXiv:2608.13696, 2026-08-13 — predates this sweep's strict window but wasn't flagged in
prior sweeps) profiles cuDF relational-operator kernels on L4 vs. GH200 and finds that a
13.4× jump in memory bandwidth produced far less than 13.4× kernel speedup — GPU database
kernels are now **instruction-bound**, not memory-bound, on current hardware. This echoes your
own finding that the join's residual cost is dominated by per-survivor instruction overhead
(shared-memory append, prefilter divisions, compaction) rather than bandwidth. Their tool
("Valk") combines multiple profilers to attribute stalls to instruction issue vs. memory;
worth replicating that attribution split on the overlap-join kernel specifically for the AND-
survivor path, to confirm whether the ~60ps/survivor cost is instruction-issue-bound (in which
case cutting instruction count per survivor, e.g. via Barrett-style division avoidance in the
mid-digit prefilter, is the higher-leverage fix) or genuinely latency-bound on shared-memory
atomics. Separately, "Including Bloom Filters in Bottom-up Optimization" (arXiv:2505.02994,
2025) and an October-2026 ShannonBase commit (GitHub, **unverified** beyond a search snippet)
adding cost-aware blocked runtime Bloom filters to hash-join probes both point at the same
cost/selectivity-ordering idea as B1's citation — the "order by cost/rejection ratio" result
is being independently re-derived in query-optimizer contexts right now, which is mild
corroboration it's the right framing for your prefilter ordering too.
